Temporal summation

A person can control the amount of force exerted by a single motor unit by controlling the frequency at which the brain signals the muscle fibres to contract. This is done by releasing pulsing action potentials.

Key terms

Latent period
The few milliseconds between the action potential and the first rise in tension, while excitation–contraction coupling happens (AP down the T-tubules, Ca²⁺ released from the SR).
Contraction phase
Tension rises as Ca²⁺ binds troponin and cross-bridges cycle.
Relaxation phase
SERCA pumps Ca²⁺ back into the SR, cross-bridges detach and tension falls back to zero.

What is happening

One action potential releases a single pulse of Ca²⁺. It is pumped away again before the cross-bridges have had time to fully stretch the elastic parts of the muscle (tendon, connective tissue, titin), so the fibre never reaches its full force.

Peak tension is only about a quarter of what the fibre can produce, and the fibre is fully relaxed again within roughly 0.2 s.

How this relates to force by will

A single twitch is the building block, but too brief and weak to be useful on its own. Because each action potential is all-or-none, the nervous system cannot make one twitch stronger. To increase force it must send impulses more often (columns 2 and 3) or recruit more motor units.

interval
peak tension

Key terms

Temporal (wave) summation
When a second action potential arrives before the fibre has relaxed, the new twitch adds on top of the remaining tension.
Stimulation (firing) frequency
Number of action potentials per second (Hz); the interval between them is 1000 ÷ frequency ms.

What is happening

  • Ca²⁺ from one impulse has not been fully pumped back before the next arrives, so more troponin stays switched on.
  • The elastic parts of the muscle are still stretched from the last twitch, so each new twitch starts higher.
  • Tension climbs to a higher, rippled plateau. Faster rates mean smaller ripples and a higher plateau. Try the slider.

How this increases force by will

To push harder, the brain makes motor neurons fire faster (rate coding). The same muscle fibres then produce more force simply because their twitches summate. Everyday steady efforts, like holding a cup or standing, use this partly-fused range.

interval
peak tension
× twitch

Key terms

Fusion frequency
The firing rate at which the ripples disappear, around 50 Hz and higher. Fast-twitch fibres need higher rates than slow-twitch fibres.
Tetanic tension
The plateau tension of a fused tetanus, about 3–4 times a single twitch. This is the most force the fibres can make.

What is happening

  • Ca²⁺ stays high the whole time, so virtually every troponin site is occupied and cross-bridges cycle continuously.
  • The elastic parts of the muscle are fully stretched, so all the cross-bridge force reaches the tendon.
  • Above the fusion frequency, firing even faster adds very little. The response has saturated.

How this increases force by will

A maximal voluntary effort (a sprint, a heavy lift) drives motor neurons to their highest rates, giving smooth maximum force from each unit. Once a unit is fused it cannot give more. Any further force must come from recruiting more motor units. Fused tetanus is also costly in ATP and fatigues quickly, so it is used only briefly.

Illustrative model: each impulse produces the same twitch shape (latent period ≈ 10 ms, peak ≈ 50 ms, relaxed by ≈ 200 ms). Overlapping twitches add up and saturate at the maximum (fused tetanic) tension, with a single twitch ≈ 25% of maximum. Impulses are delivered for the first 400 ms of each trace, then stop, so you can also see the relaxation.
