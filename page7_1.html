Curl variations: standing curl vs preacher curl

Same arm, same dumbbell, two body positions. Where in the movement is the biceps stressed the most, and how stretched is it at that moment?

Standing biceps curl

Preacher curl

What the page shows

Both exercises use the same arm and the same dumbbell. The only difference is where the upper arm points, and therefore where gravity pulls hardest. In a standing curl the upper arm hangs vertically. The dumbbell's lever arm about the elbow is largest when the forearm is horizontal, at φ = 90°, which is mid-range for the muscle.

In a preacher curl the upper arm rests on a pad tilted α forward. The forearm is horizontal at φ = 90° − α, much nearer full extension. There the biceps is long: stretched. Near the top, the forearm passes vertical and the load almost vanishes.

The biceps' own lever (its moment arm) also changes through the movement. It is shortest when the arm is straight, so a load that still pulls hard near full extension, as in the preacher curl, demands a very large muscle force there. This is why the preacher curl's peak stress sits at the bottom of the rep, while the standing curl's peak sits in mid-range.

The key graph (bottom right) plots stress against muscle strain. Each exercise reaches its peak stress at a different stretch of the same muscle.

Model and equations

Same static setup as the Elbow System page (rigid segments, quasi-static, no acceleration, all elbow-flexor force carried by the biceps), with one refinement. A straight shoulder-to-insertion line gives a moment arm that falls to zero at full extension and in deep flexion. The real biceps tendon wraps the joint, so its moment arm is smallest with the arm straight, peaks in mid-range and falls again in deep flexion (Murray et al., 1995). We use a smooth curve of that shape.

d(φ) = d0 + (dmax − d0) · sin(πφ / 200°)    moment arm

L(φ) = L90 − ∫90°φ d(φ′) dφ′    muscle length (tendon excursion: dL = −d dφ)

τ = W Lf · sin(φ + α)+    load torque (α = 0 standing)

F = τ / d    muscle force

A = A0 L90 / L    area at constant volume

σ = F / A,   ε = (L − L90)/L90

φ = elbow flexion (0° = straight arm, as in the studies). ( )+: once the forearm passes vertical the load no longer pulls the elbow open, so the biceps needs no force. d0 = 1.5 cm, dmax = 4.5 cm at φ = 100°, L90 = 30 cm, Lf = 30 cm, A0 = 4.6 cm²: illustrative values to check against the literature. Absolute stresses are high because the brachialis and brachioradialis, which share the load in reality, are ignored. The shape of each curve, and where it peaks, is what matters here.

Why peaking at different stretch is a good thing

Claim: different curl variations load the biceps hardest at different muscle lengths. Using more than one variation, and especially including one that loads the muscle when it is stretched, gives a more complete stimulus.

  1. Pedrosa et al. (2023). Nineteen untrained women did the seated dumbbell preacher curl for 8 weeks, one arm in the initial range (0–68°, muscle long) and the other in the final range (68–135°, muscle short). The long-length arm gained more biceps cross-sectional area at the distal site (70% of length; similar at 50%) and more 1RM strength.
  2. Sato et al. (2021). Training the elbow flexors at extended angles (0–50°) increased muscle thickness about 8.9%, versus about 3.4% at flexed angles (80–130°). Only the extended group gained significant strength.
  3. Kassiano et al. (2022), systematic review. Planned exercise variation, chosen on anatomical and biomechanical grounds, seems to enhance regional hypertrophy and strength; excessive random variation may compromise gains.
Caveats: small samples, mostly untrained participants, and effects that are regional and modest rather than whole-muscle. Later work in trained lifters found smaller differences.
