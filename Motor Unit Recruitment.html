Motor unit recruitment

How the nervous system grades the force of a whole muscle by choosing how many motor units to switch on. First we look at what a motor unit is, then we set up a simple mechanical model that turns the number of recruited units into a muscle force.

2 · A quasi-static model: from recruited units to muscle force

From this point onwards we deliberately simplify the muscle so that its force can be written down directly. Each assumption is listed separately so it can later be checked against published research and refined.

Assumptions

  1. No viscoelastic or time-dependent behaviour. We ignore force–velocity effects, stress relaxation, creep, activation and deactivation dynamics, fatigue and potentiation. The muscle is treated as quasi-static: every state is an equilibrium (isometric or very slow). To substantiate: conditions under which a quasi-static (isometric) description is acceptable.
  2. Two stress–strain curves only. Every muscle fibre follows exactly one of two curves. An inactive fibre follows the passive curve σp(ε). An activated fibre follows the active curve σa(ε) = σp(ε) + σiso(ε), where σiso is the active isometric (force–length) contribution. All fibres share the same two curves. To substantiate: passive and active force–length relations of skeletal muscle fibres.
  3. All-or-none recruitment of motor units. A motor unit is either fully off (all its fibres on σp) or fully on (all its fibres on σa, i.e. fused tetanus). Rate coding and partial (unfused) activation are not modelled. To substantiate: link between maximal firing rate and the active curve.
  4. Fibres are ropes (uniaxial). Each fibre carries only a tensile force along its own axis (tangential to the fibre). There is no transverse normal stress, no shear stress and no bending. Fibres cannot push, so they carry no compression: stress is zero below slack length.
  5. No lateral force transmission. Fibres do not pass force sideways to each other or to the surrounding connective tissue; their axial forces simply add up at the tendon. The connective tissue carries no load of its own (its contribution, if any, is lumped into the passive curve). To substantiate: size of lateral (myofascial) force transmission.
  6. Parallel, full-length fibres with equal strain. All fibres run parallel to the line of pull (pennation angle θ = 0) and span the muscle from tendon to tendon. They therefore all experience the same strain ε as the muscle. For a pennate muscle each force would be multiplied by cos θ.
  7. Rigid tendon. Series elasticity is ignored, so the muscle strain is set directly by the joint position.
  8. Constant volume: the area changes with strain. Muscle tissue is nearly incompressible, so a fibre that is stretched gets thinner and one that shortens gets fatter. Its current area is A(L) = V/L = A0/(1 + ε). The two curves give true (Cauchy) stress on this current area. If the muscle's cross-section also varies along its length (e.g. a fusiform belly), the area becomes a function A(x, ε) of position x and is handled numerically, slice by slice (step 6 of the derivation). To substantiate: incompressibility of muscle; whether published stress–strain data are nominal or true stress.
  9. Fibre areas add up to the physiological cross-sectional area (PCSA). Because the fibre forces add linearly (A4–A6), only the total area of a group of fibres matters. We therefore use the muscle's PCSA, the area cut perpendicular to the fibres, instead of individual fibre areas. Each motor unit owns a fraction ak of the PCSA, and Σak = 1. To substantiate: estimating PCSA from muscle volume and fibre length; fraction of PCSA occupied by fibres.
  10. Recruitment by the size principle. The nervous system switches units on from smallest to largest, stopping at the smallest set whose force meets the required external force. To substantiate: Henneman's size principle.

Derivation

1 · Force in one fibre. By A4 a fibre is a rope: it carries only an axial tension, equal to its stress times its current area.
Fi = σ(ε) · Ai(L)
2 · Equilibrium at the tendon. With no shear, no transverse stress and no lateral transfer (A4, A5), and all fibres parallel to the line of pull (A6), the muscle force is just the sum of the fibre forces. Split the fibres into the activated set 𝒜 (active curve) and the inactive set ℐ (passive curve):
F = Σi∈𝒜 σa(ε) · Ai(L) + Σj∈ℐ σp(ε) · Aj(L)
3 · Equal strain. All fibres have the same strain (A6) and the same two curves (A2), so the stresses come out of each sum:
F = σa(ε) · Aact(L) + σp(ε) · Ainact(L)
4 · Area: constant volume. Each fibre keeps its volume (A8): Ai(L) · L = A0,i · L0, so Ai(L) = A0,i/(1 + ε). The resting areas add up to the resting PCSA (A9), and the recruited units own the fraction f = Σk recruited ak of it:
Aact(L) = f · PCSA0/(1 + ε),   Ainact(L) = (1 − f) · PCSA0/(1 + ε)
5 · Muscle force. Substituting, and using σa = σp + σiso:
F(f, ε) = [PCSA0/(1 + ε)] · [ f · σa(ε) + (1 − f) · σp(ε) ] = A(L) · [ σp(ε) + f · σiso(ε) ]
The force is linear in the recruited fraction f, which is why the total PCSA is enough and individual fibre areas are not needed. If the curves are given as nominal stress (force per resting area), drop the factor 1/(1 + ε).
6 · Area that varies along the muscle, A(x). If the cross-section changes with position x along the muscle, the rope assumption still gives the same force at every cross-section (dF/dx = 0). The stress, and so the strain, then varies with x. Cut the muscle into n thin slices of resting length Δxm and solve each one numerically:
F = [A0(xm)/(1 + εm)] · [ σp(εm) + f · σiso(εm) ]   for every slice m,   ΔL = Σm εm · Δxm
For a given muscle length, adjust F until the slice elongations add up to the imposed ΔL. For a uniform area this collapses back to step 5.
7 · How the brain picks the number of units. Rearranging step 5 for a required external force Freq at the current strain gives the recruited fraction needed:
freq = [ Freq · (1 + ε)/PCSA0 − σp(ε) ] / σiso(ε)
The nervous system recruits units in size order, 1, 2, 3, …, until Σak ≥ freq (A10). If freq ≤ 0 the passive tissue alone is enough; if freq > 1 the force is beyond what the muscle can produce at this length. Small units come first, so force can be adjusted finely at low effort, and big jumps come only when high force is needed.
Symbols: F force on the tendon (N) · ε = (L − L0)/L0 muscle (= fibre) strain, L0 optimal length · σp, σa passive and active true stress (kPa), σiso = σa − σp · PCSA0 resting physiological cross-sectional area · A(L) = PCSA0/(1 + ε) current area · ak share of the PCSA owned by motor unit k · f recruited fraction.
What this model leaves out (on purpose): twitch dynamics and temporal summation (see the Temporal summation page), rate coding, force–velocity, fatigue, pennation, tendon stretch and lateral force transmission. These can be added back once the base model is checked against data.
