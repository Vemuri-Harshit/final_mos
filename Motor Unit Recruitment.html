Motor unit recruitment

Each muscle fibre exists in one of two states, activated and inactivated. Each have their own stress-strain curves with an activated fibre exerting higher stress at a given value of strain. The total force exerted by the muscle is the sum of the forces exerted by both the activated and inactivated muscle fibres. By increasing the number of fibres activated, the muscle can exert more force.

Motor unit: one motor neuron together with all of the muscle fibres it innervates. It is the smallest unit of force that the nervous system can switch on: when the neuron fires, every one of its fibres contracts together (all-or-none).
  1. No viscoelastic or time-dependent behaviour. We ignore force–velocity effects, stress relaxation, creep, activation and deactivation dynamics, fatigue and potentiation. The muscle is treated as quasi-static: every state is an equilibrium (isometric or very slow).
  2. Two stress–strain curves only. Every muscle fibre follows exactly one of two curves, and all fibres share the same two curves (ε = 0 is the optimal length; stresses in kPa):
    passive (inactive fibre):  σp(ε) = Kp (eε/ε0 − 1) for ε > 0,  σp(ε) = 0 for ε ≤ 0
    active isometric part:  σiso(ε) = σmax e−(ε/w)²
    active (activated fibre):  σa(ε) = σp(ε) + σiso(ε)
    Constants: Kp = 3.1 kPa, ε0 = 0.12, σmax = 225 kPa (22.5 N/cm², the peak at ε = 0), w = 0.35. These are illustrative values, to be replaced by values from the literature.
  3. All-or-none recruitment of motor units. A motor unit is either fully off (all its fibres on σp) or fully on (all its fibres on σa, i.e. fused tetanus). Rate coding and partial (unfused) activation are not modelled.
  4. Fibres are ropes (uniaxial). Each fibre carries only a tensile force along its own axis. There is no transverse normal stress, no shear stress and no bending. Fibres cannot push, so they carry no compression: the passive stress is zero below slack length (ε ≤ 0).
  5. No lateral force transmission. Fibres do not pass force sideways to each other or to the surrounding connective tissue; their axial forces simply add up at the tendon. The connective tissue carries no load of its own (its contribution, if any, is lumped into the passive curve).
  6. Parallel, full-length fibres with equal strain. All fibres run parallel to the line of pull (pennation angle θ = 0) and span the muscle from tendon to tendon, so they all have the same strain ε as the muscle. For a pennate muscle each force would be multiplied by cos θ.
  7. Rigid tendon. Series elasticity is ignored, so the muscle strain is set directly by the joint position.
  8. Constant volume: the area changes with strain. Muscle tissue is nearly incompressible, so its current area is A(L) = V/L = PCSA0/(1 + ε). The two curves give true (Cauchy) stress on this current area. The calculator can switch this off (constant area, A = PCSA0) for comparison. If the cross-section also varies along the length, the area becomes A(x, ε) and is handled slice by slice (step 6 of the derivation).
  9. Fibre areas add up to the physiological cross-sectional area (PCSA). Because fibre forces add linearly (assumptions 4–6), only the total area of a group of fibres matters. The muscle has N = 10 motor units; unit k owns the share
    ak = rk−1 / (1 + r + … + r9),  r = 1.45,  k = 1, …, 10,  Σ ak = 1
    so a1 ≈ 0.011 and a10 ≈ 0.318: the largest unit is about 28 times (1.459) the smallest. The fibres of each unit are scattered through the cross-section.
  10. Recruitment by the size principle. The nervous system switches units on from smallest to largest (k = 1, 2, 3, …), stopping at the smallest set whose force meets the required external force.
  11. Ranges of the inputs (calculator sliders).
    strain ε: −0.30 to +0.45 (step 0.01, starts at 0)
    required force Freq: 0 to 520 N (step 1 N, starts at 150 N)
    resting area PCSA0: 5 to 40 cm² (step 1 cm², starts at 20 cm²)
    Units: 1 kPa · 1 cm² = 0.1 N. For example, with PCSA0 = 20 cm² at ε = 0 the largest possible force is 20 × 225 × 0.1 = 450 N.

Left out on purpose: twitch dynamics and temporal summation (see the Temporal summation page), rate coding, force–velocity, fatigue, pennation, tendon stretch and lateral force transmission.

1. Force in one fibre (assumption 4):

Fi = σ(ε) Ai(L)

2. Equilibrium at the tendon (assumptions 4–6), with 𝒜 the activated fibres and ℐ the inactive fibres:

F = Σi∈𝒜 σa(ε) Ai(L) + Σj∈ℐ σp(ε) Aj(L)

3. Equal strain and identical curves (assumptions 2, 6):

F = σa(ε) Aact(L) + σp(ε) Ainact(L)

4. Constant volume (assumption 8) and PCSA (assumption 9):

Ai(L) L = A0,i L0  ⇒  Ai(L) = A0,i / (1 + ε)

f = Σk recruited ak

Aact(L) = f PCSA0 / (1 + ε)

Ainact(L) = (1 − f) PCSA0 / (1 + ε)

5. Substituting, with σa = σp + σiso:

F(f, ε) = [PCSA0 / (1 + ε)] [ f σa(ε) + (1 − f) σp(ε) ]

F(f, ε) = A(L) [ σp(ε) + f σiso(ε) ]

F is linear in f. For nominal stress (force per resting area), drop the factor 1/(1 + ε).

6. Area varying along the muscle, A(x). The same F acts at every section (dF/dx = 0). For slices m = 1, …, n of resting length Δxm:

F = [A0(xm) / (1 + εm)] [ σp(εm) + f σiso(εm) ]   for every m

ΔL = Σm εm Δxm

F is adjusted until the slice elongations add up to the imposed ΔL. For uniform area this reduces to step 5.

7. Recruited fraction for a required force Freq, from step 5:

freq = [ Freq (1 + ε) / PCSA0 − σp(ε) ] / σiso(ε)

recruit k = 1, 2, 3, … until a1 + … + ak ≥ freq

If freq ≤ 0 the passive tissue alone is enough; if freq > 1 the force cannot be produced at this length.

Symbols: F tendon force (N); ε = (L − L0)/L0 strain, L0 optimal length; σp, σa passive and active true stress (kPa); σiso = σa − σp; PCSA0 resting physiological cross-sectional area; A(L) = PCSA0/(1 + ε) current area; ak share of the PCSA owned by unit k; f recruited fraction.
